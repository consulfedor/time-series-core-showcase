    MEMORY · ATTENTION · MNEMONICS

    A memory like an elephant

    Attention shapes our memory.
    An image helps connect a familiar meaning with a new word.

    A plumber is dancing in the bathroom. He has a wrench in one hand and a tub of ice cream in the other. He smears the ice cream across his shoulders, shivers at the cold and keeps dancing. White streaks cover his work clothes.

    The scene contains a cue for the English word plumber: the Russian word for this ice cream, пломбир, plombir. The ice cream suggests the sound; the person with the wrench supplies the meaning. A mnemonic joins them in one event, giving you a scene to recall when you need the word. [2]

    ONE APARTMENT. FIVE PLACES. UNEXPECTED GUESTS.

    Imagine you are at home

    An image can also have an address. A familiar apartment, where you can find the kitchen and the front door without thinking, will be the setting for our next story. Strange things will happen along the way. Their purpose will become clear at the end.

    This story is by Konstantin Dudin. Walk from the entrance through the hallway and living room, into the kitchen, then to the bathroom. Picture it happening in your own apartment, with its doors, floor and table. The explanation is closed for now.

    Entrance·Hallway·Living room·Kitchen·Bathroom

    A mother bear and her cub scratch at the door

    You approach the front door and hear scratching. Outside, a mother bear and her cub are trying to get in: a large paw scrapes the door, and a small one taps beside it. Both are insisting that you let them in.

    Hercules oat porridge covers the hallway floor

    You turn around. The hallway floor is covered in porridge made from Hercules oats, a familiar Russian name for rolled oats. Thick flakes spread beneath your feet. The warm, sticky porridge clings to your heels. You have to step over it to get through.

    Twin sisters dance on the living-room table

    Music is coming from the living room. Two adult twin sisters are performing a sexy, energetic dance right on the table. Identical faces and synchronised movements; the table shakes beneath their feet. You walk around the dancing twins and head into the kitchen.

    A frosted crown and northern lights in the kitchen

    A crown stands on the kitchen table, covered in frost. Its points sparkle, and the northern lights shimmer above it. Bands of colour move beneath the ceiling. You reach towards the crown and feel the cold on your fingers.

    A cash register on the bathroom door: sing to get in

    You reach the bathroom, but the door is locked. A cash register is mounted right on the door. You look for a banknote slot, but there is none: a microphone takes its place. To get in, you have to sing. You sing into the microphone, the lock clicks, and the door opens.

    What have we just remembered?

    Six constellations of the northern sky.

    Each name now has a scene and an address. Let us walk through the same places:

    Entrance: the mother bear and cub
    Ursa Major and Ursa Minor: the Great Bear and Little Bear.
    Hallway: Hercules oat porridge
    Hercules: a familiar Russian name for rolled oats.
    Living room: twin sisters dancing on the table
    Gemini: the Twins, pictured directly.
    Kitchen: a frosted crown beneath the northern lights
    Corona Borealis: a crown and an image of the north.
    Bathroom: a cash register and microphone on the door
    Cassiopeia: Russian “kassa” (cash register) and “pet’” (to sing) provide an approximate sound cue.

    Now close the explanation, walk through the apartment in your mind and name all six constellations. The bears at the door, the porridge in the hallway: recall the scene and say its name.

    You can then find these six constellations on a star chart.

    The apartment provides an order: through the door, along the hallway, into the living room. Something different happens in each place. Scratching claws, sticky porridge underfoot, a cold crown: each scene has details to hold on to. Return to the door in your mind and see whether you can follow the whole route.

    Memory begins with attention. If another task distracts us while we are learning words, those words become harder to recall later. [1] Here, attention rests on what is happening: a sound, a movement, an unexpected detail.

    FROM AN IMAGE TO AN ENGLISH WORD

    Connecting meaning with sound

    You recognise a carrot at a glance. You can imagine its colour, its crunch and its slightly sweet taste. For a Russian learner, the meaning is already familiar; the English word carrot is what needs to be learned. A picture of a carrot shows the object, but does not yet suggest its new name.

    The keyword method adds a sound cue to that familiar meaning. Listen to the foreign word, find a familiar word with a similar sound, and bring the two images together in an action. [2] For carrot, we will use the Russian карета, kareta, a carriage.

    An enormous carrot is being pushed into a tiny carriage. It jams in the doorway, the wheels sag, and its green top sticks out of the window. The carriage starts moving, and the carrot bounces over the cobbles. When you need the English name, picture the carrot in the carriage. The familiar kareta suggests the sound of carrot.

    If you remember only the dance, make the ice cream more noticeable.

    Each scene has its own noticeable detail. The carrot crunches as it jams in the carriage door; cold ice cream trickles down the plumber's collar. Such details help distinguish the images. [3] Choose details that come readily to your own imagination.

    The sound cue is approximate; the recording supplies the pronunciation. Listen to plumber: the stress falls on the first syllable and the b is silent. Repeat the word while picturing the plumber. The ice cream helps you recall it; the spoken model tells you how to say it.

    Look away from the text and recall the English word for сантехник. Recall plombir and say the whole word. Reverse the direction: when you hear plumber, whom do you picture? Understanding a spoken word and recalling it for your own answer are two different tasks; both deserve practice. [4] [5]

    “We need a plumber.” That is the kind of sentence you will need in a conversation. The imagined scene helps you find the word; the sentence gives you a reason to say it.

    WHAT WILL REMAIN TOMORROW?

    “I know it”—while the answer is in view

    Look at a familiar word, and its translation comes immediately. Hide the text, and the answer disappears. This is a useful moment to check whether you can recall it yourself. Give yourself a few seconds, then reveal the answer and compare. Such attempts to recall help retain what you have learned. [6]

    Half an hour later, try naming the profession in English again. Then in the evening, the next day and a few days after that. Each time, attempt it before looking at the answer. Recall attempts spread over time help retain what you have learned for longer. [8] Here is a schedule to begin with:

    30 min→3 h→8 h→1 day→3 days→9 days→27 days

    All times are measured from the end of the first session. [7]

    If a word slips away, work out what is missing. Can you picture the plumber but not recall the English name? Return to the ice cream and listen to the recording. Did the word come easily? Schedule the next check for later. Return to difficult words sooner; you can adjust the schedule to your answers.

    PIMSLEUR: YOUR ANSWER FIRST

    The pause that asks you to speak

    A question plays in an audio lesson, followed by silence. The answer has not been given. You have to build your reply before the speaker continues. Paul Pimsleur calls this anticipation in How to Learn a Foreign Language. [9]

    Another of his ideas was to return to learned material after increasingly long gaps. In A Memory Schedule (1967), Pimsleur described a schedule in which material reappears while the time between encounters gradually grows. [10]

    A question. A pause for your answer. A spoken model to compare it with. Another encounter with the word later.

    During the pause after a question, you search your memory for the word. At the next encounter, you try to recall it again. Researchers continued to study retrieval and spaced practice after Pimsleur. [6] [8] Our English practice adds text and imagery to the audio, so you can hear the word, make sense of it and attempt an answer.

    ATTENDING TO DIFFERENT ASPECTS OF ONE WORD

    Hear it, see it, say it

    In the English practice, words are highlighted as the recording moves on, like karaoke. You hear the sentence and see where the spoken word sits on the line. This lets you connect its sound with its spelling; reading with audio is one way to encounter new vocabulary. [11] Once the reply makes sense, look away and try answering without the text.

    Say your answer aloud. Hearing yourself in another language can feel awkward at first. Repeat after the speaker, then say the sentence independently. You will hear your own pronunciation and the places where you still hesitate.

    Cold ice cream, a ridiculous dance or a singing cash register can make you smile or surprise you. Emotion influences memory. [12] For this task, a particularly useful detail brings back the word along with that feeling.

    Sound, spelling, meaning, imagery and your own answer. They all belong to the same word. Highlighting helps you follow the speech, imagery connects sound with meaning, and a question prompts you to search for an answer. Each technique directs attention towards a different part of the task.

    The whole cycle

    Purpose→Attention→Understanding→Connections→Recall→Repetition→Use→Check

    You need to ask for butter at the table. You listen to butter, notice its sound and picture it melting on the radiator. Later, you try to recall the word and say the whole request. If you stumble, you can see what to revisit: listen to the recording, recover the image or practise the sentence.

    Now return to the apartment door. Who is scratching there? What scene awaits in the kitchen? Your attention has been on English words for a while. See what remains of the first story.
